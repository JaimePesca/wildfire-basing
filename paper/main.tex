\begin{abstract}
Aerial suppression productivity is governed by a three-leg cycle: an
aircraft positions once from base to fire, then repeats the
fire-to-water refill leg on every drop, so water point location, not
base-to-fire distance alone, drives delivered water. We introduce the
cycle-constrained aerial suppression base and water point location
problem, the first to site bases and water refill points jointly with
cycle-endogenous productivity, as a two-stage stochastic program with
a mean-risk objective based on conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), an
exact linearization, and a fix-and-optimize matheuristic whose reduced
subproblems are exact. An open-data case
study for Cundinamarca, Colombia, built around the country's Firehawk
acquisition, shows a fleet-limited-first, physics-limited-second
regime: budget dominates, uncalibrated growth constants act as a
containability cliff, sequential bases-first planning matches the
integrated optimum wherever certification is possible, and
tractability collapses where fleets and budget slack grow, a regime
the matheuristic still searches effectively, albeit without
certificates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Colombia's eastern Andean department of Cundinamarca leads the
country's historical record of reported forest fires, with 7,908
events reported to the national risk management databases between
1921 and April 2020, nearly twice those of the second-ranked
department \citep{ungrdnd}. The extreme fire episode of January 2024
around Bogot{\'a} prompted the declaration of a national disaster
\citep{decreto2024}, and aerial means carried the weight of the
emergency response, more than 755 flight hours and 2,170 water
discharges by late February \citep{ungrd2024}. In its wake, the
Colombian Aerospace Force (FAC, Fuerza Aeroespacial Colombiana) and
the National Unit for Disaster Risk Management (UNGRD, Unidad
Nacional para la Gesti{\'o}n del Riesgo de Desastres) committed 150
billion Colombian pesos (COP, with billion denoting $10^9$
throughout) to acquire two Sikorsky S-70 Firehawk helicopters
\citep{fac2025}, reported as the first of their class in Latin
America \citep{infodefensa2025}. A country buying firefighting
aircraft faces
an immediate strategic question that current models do not answer:
where should the supporting bases and, critically, the water refill
points be located, and how should a single budget be split among bases,
aircraft, and water infrastructure?

The operational physics of helicopter suppression make this a joint
question. An aircraft flies from its base to the fire once, to
position, and then cycles between the fire and the nearest usable water
point for every drop. As the number of drops grows, total delivered
water is dominated by the fire-to-water leg, which is paid on every
cycle, not by the base-to-fire leg, which is paid once. Aircraft
productivity is therefore endogenous to the joint configuration of
bases and water points. A classical facility location model built on
base-to-fire distance alone, of the p-median family
\citep{mladenovic2007}, cannot represent this tradeoff, and, as
Section~\ref{sec:lit} shows, no existing model sites bases and water
points jointly with cycle-endogenous productivity.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we formulate the
cycle-constrained aerial suppression base and water point location
problem as a two-stage stochastic program: first-stage decisions open
bases, station aircraft, and enable water points under a single budget;
second-stage recourse dispatches aircraft to fires scenario by
scenario. The objective is a mean-risk convex combination of expected
loss and conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) \citep{rockafellar2000}. Fire
containment is physical: a fire is contained if the liters delivered
within a critical window exceed a requirement that grows with arrival
time and rate of spread, so no historical suppression outcome data,
which do not exist for Colombia, are needed. The only nonlinearity, a
bilinear product of dispatch and refill assignment, admits an exact
McCormick linearization \citep{gupte2013}, yielding a mixed-integer
linear program (MILP).

Second, we develop a fix-and-optimize matheuristic
\citep{helber2010} with large neighborhood search \citep{pisinger2010}
over the first-stage variables. Each iteration solves a reduced
sub-MILP that is exact by construction, over a geographic neighborhood
of candidate sites plus the incumbent's open sites; a probabilistic
random destroy operator, standard in adaptive large neighborhood
search \citep{ropke2006}, provably escapes a class of local optima
that purely geographic neighborhoods cannot leave. The reduced
sub-problem keeps the full scenario set, so, by construction, every
accepted move is a feasible first-stage solution whose true objective
improves the incumbent (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

Third, we construct a fully sourced, replicable case study for
Cundinamarca from open data only: satellite fire detections for all of
2024 from the Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS)
of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), clustered
into events with spatio-temporal density-based spatial clustering of
applications with noise (ST-DBSCAN) \citep{birant2007}; land cover,
slope, and wind from European Space Agency (ESA) WorldCover, the
Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM), and NASA Prediction of
Worldwide Energy Resources (POWER);
population exposure from WorldPop, cross-checked against the Colombian
census; 120 candidate bases from the national aerodrome index and
5,449 candidate water points from OpenStreetMap and the regional water
authority; and the real Firehawk's cost, tank capacity, and cruise
speed. Aircraft parameters are taken from the manufacturer's
specification and the acquisition program's published figures;
parameters that lack a calibrated Colombian source enter as explicit,
disclosed assumptions and are explored over ranges in the sensitivity
analysis.

Fourth, our computational study yields policy-relevant structural
findings rather than only benchmark numbers. Under the real program
budget the deployment is fleet-limited first and physics-limited
second: one Firehawk absorbs half the budget and serves at most one
fire per day-scenario, budget moves the objective most, and the
uncalibrated containment constants act as a containability cliff
that decides whether that aircraft buys anything at all, while site
costs never move the objective across their documented ranges. A
best-case sequential planner that sites bases first and water second
matches the integrated optimum in every configuration we can
certify, one and two aircraft alike, and the strongest integrated
search available finds nothing better where certification fails; we
explain structurally why. Conversely, the direct MILP loses
tractability exactly where the integrated question becomes
interesting, in multi-aircraft, high-budget-slack regimes, which is
what motivates the matheuristic.

The paper moves from the gap to the policy reading.
Section~\ref{sec:lit} locates the three conditions no existing model
meets jointly, which the formulation of Section~\ref{sec:model} is
then built to satisfy; Section~\ref{sec:data} assembles the open-data
Cundinamarca instance on which the model is solved;
Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the matheuristic that carries the
model beyond the direct solver's reach; Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
derives the structural findings above from the computational study;
and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} closes with the limitations and the
questions the results leave open.

\section{Related work and gap}
\label{sec:lit}

Aerial resource location for wildfire suppression began with airtanker
base selection: \citet{hodgson1978} chose bases with location-allocation
models for one-strike initial attack, and \citet{maclellan1996} based
airtankers for Ontario with an expected-cost model over historical
demand. Modern strategic deployment models keep this base-side focus:
\citet{skold2024} locate aerial firefighting resources in Sweden with a
binary program over wildfire risk data, and \citet{mendes2023} place
wildland fire suppression resources on a landscape with an iterated
local search metaheuristic benchmarked against exact mixed-integer
programming solutions. Stochastic treatments deploy
suppression resources across existing stations under fire-occurrence
uncertainty: the scenario-based standard-response models of
\citet{haight2007}, the simulation plus stochastic integer programming
approach of \citet{ntaimo2013}, the two-stage seasonal deployment and
dispatch design of \citet{wei2015}, which is the closest methodological
comparator to our second stage, and the robust prepositioning of
\citet{mendes2025}. \citet{huang2025} go further on the base side,
siting new aviation rescue stations and configuring a heterogeneous
helicopter fleet with a two-stage multi-objective stochastic model
driven by satellite-based fire probabilities, yet with no water
infrastructure and with losses driven by one-way response time rather
than a refill cycle. Recent work also allocates heterogeneous aerial
fleets across capacity-constrained airbases under efficiency and equity
objectives \citep{tasias2026}. In all of these, water is either absent
or exogenous.

A separate stream sites water infrastructure, with the aircraft side
fixed: \citet{ramalho2021} and \citet{ramalho2024} allocate water
reservoirs by fire risk with geotechnological methods, and
\citet{alfaro2024} optimize reservoir placement for helicopter
transport in Valpara{\'i}so. Operational models capture the refill
cycle but take infrastructure as given: \citet{rodriguezveiga2018}
assign aircraft to flight routes and refueling points during
suppression, \citet{rodriguezbarreiro2026} plan helicopter circuits
including water loading points for a given fire, and
\citet{skorinkapov2024} schedule aerial resources against zone-assigned
water loading points. Closest in scope, \citet{maras2023} site both
heliports and water sources with GIS-based multi-objective programming,
but in two separate deterministic models with fixed response-time
thresholds, without a refill cycle and without uncertainty.

Table~\ref{tab:gap} classifies this literature against the three
conditions that jointly define the gap: (a) joint siting of bases and
water points in one model, (b) aircraft productivity endogenous to the
base to fire to water cycle, and (c) strategic, pre-season decisions
under uncertainty. Reading the table by column isolates the gap.
Cycle-dependent productivity appears only at the operational level,
where no siting takes place; joint siting appears in no prior model;
and no work combines cycle-dependent productivity with strategic,
uncertainty-aware siting. The proposed model is the only row satisfying
all three, under stochastic programming with a mean-risk CVaR
objective. The nearest works each own a single pillar:
\citet{maras2023} site both facility types but separately,
deterministically, and without a cycle; \citet{rodriguezbarreiro2026}
capture the cycle, and even select loading points, but operationally,
with given infrastructure; and \citet{wei2015} are strategic and
stochastic but site no water and use coverage rather than a refill
cycle.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Setting and notation}

A planner chooses, before the fire season, which candidate aerial bases
to open, how many aircraft to station at each, and which candidate
water refill points to enable, under a single budget. Fire days then
realize randomly. On a fire day, aircraft are dispatched from open
bases to fires; an aircraft serving a fire refills at exactly one
enabled water point for the whole episode, and the water it delivers
within the critical suppression window is determined by the three-leg
cycle geometry. A fire whose delivered water reaches a physical
requirement is contained; otherwise it escapes and its exposed
population counts as loss.

Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarizes the notation.
Distances are in meters in the national planimetric coordinate
reference system (CRS), registered as code 9377 in the European
Petroleum Survey Group (EPSG) geodetic registry (EPSG:9377); time is
in hours, and money is in Colombian pesos (COP).

\subsection{Cycle productivity and physical containment}

For every $i$, $f$, $k$, $m$, and $s$, the following quantities are
parameters, computable offline, because every input to them is a
parameter under the discrete candidate-site, single-aircraft-type
scope:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{cyc}_{fkm} &= 2\,\tau^{\mathrm{fw}}_{fk} + \delta, \\
d_{ifkm} &= \max\!\Big(0,\ \Big\lfloor \tfrac{W - \tau^{\mathrm{bf}}_{if}}{\mathrm{cyc}_{fkm}} \Big\rfloor\Big), \\
L_{ifkm} &= d_{ifkm}\, Q_m,
\end{align}
so $L_{ifkm}$ is the liters one aircraft of type $m$ delivers to fire
$f$ when dispatched from base $i$ and refilling at point $k$: it flies
the positioning leg once, then completes as many refill cycles as fit
in the remaining window. A base too far to complete even one round trip
contributes zero drops. This is the formal core of the cycle insight:
$\tau^{\mathrm{bf}}_{if}$ enters once, through the numerator, while
$\tau^{\mathrm{fw}}_{fk}$ divides the window, so water geometry scales
productivity multiplicatively. Three conventions are implicit in the
drop count and disclosed here: the aircraft is assumed to arrive at the
fire with an empty tank, so its first drop, like every later one, costs
a full refill cycle (an aircraft departing loaded would gain at most
one drop; we take the conservative count); a single cruise speed
covers loaded and unloaded legs, matching the single sourced speed
specification; and flight time carries no operating cost in the
objective, so dispatch is limited by the fleet and the budget, never
by fuel accounting.

Containment is physical. Fire $f$ is contained if delivered liters
reach
\begin{equation}
R_f = c\, A_0\, e^{\rho_f t^{\mathrm{arr}}_f},
\label{eq:requirement}
\end{equation}
an exponential area growth form adopted as a phenomenological
simplification for tractability, with $c$ in liters per square meter
and $A_0$ in square meters ($A_0$ is quoted in hectares throughout the
paper and converted exactly once in the implementation). We disclose
its provenance precisely: accelerating phenomenological growth curves
are the standard design-fire description in structural fire safety
engineering \citep{nilsson2014}; wildland-specific growth models are
instead typically elliptical, propagating a fire front
\citep{sullivan2009}; and observed wildland growth rates vary by
orders of magnitude with fuel and weather \citep{balch2024}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:requirement} is therefore a modeling choice that
fits a single scalar $\rho_f$ per fire, not a wildland-validated law. Its
constants $A_0$ and $c$ have no Colombia-specific calibration source
and are treated as swept sensitivity parameters throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:exp6}). The arrival time $t^{\mathrm{arr}}_f$ is
defined exogenously as the travel time from the closest candidate base,
$\min_{i \in I} \tau^{\mathrm{bf}}_{if}$, so that $R_f$ remains a pure
per-fire parameter independent of the dispatch decision.

The requirement and the drop count deliberately run on two different
clocks, which we state explicitly. $R_f$ sizes the fire at the
earliest feasible attack time, detection plus $\min_i
\tau^{\mathrm{bf}}_{if}$; the drop count $d_{ifkm}$ measures the
window from dispatch, charging each base its full positioning leg.
When the dispatching base is not the closest candidate, the fire has
grown for longer than $t^{\mathrm{arr}}_f$, so the requirement that
dispatch faces is understated; we accept this conservative-window,
optimistic-requirement pairing rather than endogenize it, because a
dispatch-dependent requirement would become $R_{if}$ and couple the
containment threshold to the recourse decision, breaking the
parameter-only precomputation that keeps the model linear.

\subsection{Two-stage mean-risk formulation}
\label{sec:milp}

With $M_m = \lfloor B / c^{\mathrm{air}}_m \rfloor$ a budget-derived
fleet bound, the model is:
\begin{align}
\min\ & (1-\lambda) \sum_{s \in S} p_s\, \ell_s
        + \lambda \Big( \eta + \tfrac{1}{1-\alpha} \sum_{s \in S} p_s\, t_s \Big)
\label{eq:objective} \\
\text{s.t.}\
& \sum_{i \in I} c^{\mathrm{base}}_i y_i
  + \sum_{i \in I}\sum_{m \in M} c^{\mathrm{air}}_m n_{im}
  + \sum_{k \in K} c^{\mathrm{wat}}_k w_k \le B,
\label{eq:budget} \\
& n_{im} \le M_m\, y_i, \quad \forall i, m,
\label{eq:openbase} \\
& z_{fks} \le w_k, \quad \forall f, k, s,
\label{eq:openwater} \\
& \textstyle\sum_{k \in K} z_{fks} \le 1, \quad \forall f, s,
\label{eq:onewater} \\
& \textstyle\sum_{f \in F_s} x_{ifms} \le n_{im}, \quad \forall i, m, s,
\label{eq:fleet} \\
& q_{fs} = \sum_{i \in I}\sum_{k \in K}\sum_{m \in M} L_{ifkm}\, u_{ifkms},
  \quad \forall f, s,
\label{eq:delivered} \\
& u_{ifkms} \le x_{ifms}, \quad \forall i,f,k,m,s,
\label{eq:mc1} \\
& u_{ifkms} \le M_m\, z_{fks}, \quad \forall i,f,k,m,s,
\label{eq:mc2} \\
& u_{ifkms} \ge x_{ifms} - M_m (1 - z_{fks}), \quad \forall i,f,k,m,s,
\label{eq:mc3} \\
& q_{fs} \ge R_f (1 - e_{fs}), \quad \forall f, s,
\label{eq:containment} \\
& \ell_s = \textstyle\sum_{f \in F_s} D_f\, e_{fs}, \quad \forall s,
\label{eq:loss} \\
& t_s \ge \ell_s - \eta, \quad \forall s,
\label{eq:cvar} \\
& y_i, w_k, z_{fks}, e_{fs} \in \{0,1\},\ n_{im}, x_{ifms} \in \mathbb{Z}_{+},\
  q_{fs}, \ell_s, t_s, u_{ifkms} \ge 0,\ \eta \ \text{free}.
\nonumber
\end{align}

The objective \eqref{eq:objective} is the mean-risk convex combination
of expected loss and CVaR in the linear reformulation of
\citet{rockafellar2000}; $\lambda = 0$ is risk neutral and $\lambda =
1$ is pure CVaR. Constraint \eqref{eq:budget} is the single budget
covering bases, fleet, and water points, which makes the budget split
endogenous. Constraints \eqref{eq:openbase} to \eqref{eq:fleet} link
stationing to open bases, refills to enabled points, one refill point
per served fire, and dispatch to the stationed fleet. The delivered
liters coupling is bilinear in the natural formulation, $q_{fs} =
\sum L_{ifkm}\, x_{ifms} z_{fks}$; constraints \eqref{eq:mc1} to
\eqref{eq:mc3} replace each product with the auxiliary $u_{ifkms}$
through a McCormick envelope that is exact, not a relaxation, because
$z_{fks}$ is binary and $x_{ifms} \le M_m$ is valid
\citep{gupte2013}. Constraint
\eqref{eq:containment} enforces physical containment, and
\eqref{eq:loss} to \eqref{eq:cvar} define scenario loss and the CVaR
linking. Scenario probabilities must sum to one; the mean-risk CVaR
objective is otherwise unbounded, a subtle failure mode we document
because it silently arises when subsampling scenario sets without
renormalizing.

Section~\ref{sec:exp1} verifies computationally that the linearized
model and the literal bilinear program solved as a nonconvex
mixed-integer quadratically constrained program (MIQCP) return
identical optimal values on real instances. The
auxiliary count $|I| \times |K|$ per fire and scenario is what makes
the full 5,449-point water catalog intractable for the direct MILP and
motivates the matheuristic of Section~\ref{sec:method}.

The formulation treats each scenario as a single short episode with no
aircraft reuse across fires over time: constraint \eqref{eq:fleet}
caps total dispatches per scenario at the stationed fleet. With
day-length scenarios and a two-hour window this understates what one
aircraft could fly in a full day, a deliberate simplification whose
consequences we quantify and discuss in
Sections~\ref{sec:exp6} and~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

\subsection{Fire events and scenarios}

Fire events come from NASA FIRMS Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer
Suite (VIIRS) 375 m active fire detections
\citep{firms,schroeder2014} for the full calendar year 2024 over a
Cundinamarca
bounding box: 4,719 raw detections reduce to 4,470 after coordinate
validation, exact deduplication, and a nominal-confidence filter, and
are clustered into 2,425 events with ST-DBSCAN \citep{birant2007},
using $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{spatial}} = 850$ m, anchored on the 375 m
sensor resolution, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{temporal}} = 1.5$ days,
anchored on revisit and persistence; sensitivity of the event catalog
to both radii is part of the pipeline. Detections are projected to
EPSG:9377, the national single-zone planimetric standard, before
clustering. Our implementation adapts \citet{birant2007} in the way
standard across public ST-DBSCAN implementations: the original's
non-spatial attribute threshold and discrete temporal-neighbor
prefilter become a single continuous temporal radius intersected with
the spatial one, and its cluster-mean similarity gate is omitted;
border points join the first cluster that reaches them, as in the
original.

A scenario is one historical calendar day, matching the stochastic
unit of \citet{wei2015}: the 366-day pool of 2024 (282 days with at
least one event) is bootstrap-resampled with replacement into $|S| =
200$ equiprobable day scenarios under sample average approximation
(SAA) \citep{kleywegt2001}. Day scenarios keep the episode structure of
constraint \eqref{eq:fleet} valid and assume fleet availability resets
daily; cross-day dynamics are not modeled. The satellite record misses
cloud-obscured fires and detects active burning rather than ignition,
caveats inherited by any FIRMS-based catalog.

\subsection{Fire attributes}

Each fire's rate-of-spread parameter follows a land cover base rate
modified by slope and wind,
\begin{equation}
\rho_f = \rho_{\mathrm{scale}} \cdot r_{\mathrm{class}(f)} \cdot
e^{0.05\, \mathrm{slope}_f} \cdot (1 + 0.1\, \mathrm{wind}_f),
\label{eq:ros}
\end{equation}
with land cover class from ESA WorldCover 10 m \citep{worldcover},
slope in degrees from SRTM 30 m \citep{srtm,farr2007} by Horn's
finite-difference method \citep{tang2011}, and wind in meters per
second from NASA POWER
\citep{nasapower} at each fire's location and date. The relative rates
$r_{\mathrm{class}}$ are ordinally grounded in the standard fire
behavior fuel models of \citet{scott2005}, grass fastest, shrub
intermediate and strongly load-dependent, timber litter slower,
non-burnable classes spreading none; their absolute magnitude has no
Colombian calibration source, so the overall scale
$\rho_{\mathrm{scale}}$ is a swept sensitivity parameter. The
exponential slope response follows the exponential slope corrections
of the Australian McArthur-type models, as reviewed by
\citet{sharples2008}, with an illustrative coefficient; the slope
factor in Rothermel's own model is instead a tangent-squared form
\citep{andrews2018}. The value at risk $D_f$ is the WorldPop
2020 constrained population count \citep{worldpop} within 1 km of the
fire, an unmonetized exposure index cross-checked at municipal level
against the 2018 census \citep{dane2018}.

\subsection{Candidate sites and aircraft}

The 120 candidate bases combine the national civil aerodrome index
\citep{aerocivil2025} with military installations; the 5,449 candidate
water points combine named OpenStreetMap water bodies \citep{osm} with
the lagoon layer of the regional water authority (Corporaci{\'o}n
Aut{\'o}noma Regional de Cundinamarca, CAR) \citep{car2026}. The
aircraft is the Sikorsky S-70 Firehawk that Colombia is acquiring
\citep{fac2025}: 75 billion COP per unit (the program price of 150
billion for two), a 1{,}000 gallon (3{,}785 liter) tank with snorkel
refill in under one minute \citep{lockheedfirehawk} from water at
least 45 cm deep, which is what makes lagoon-scale water points
usable, and a nominal 150 knot cruise (277.8 km per hour), the figure
most consistently reported by secondary sources, since the
manufacturer publishes no cruise speed. The per-cycle overhead $\delta$ is the
sourced sub-minute refill plus an assumed 30 second drop and
positioning allowance.

\section{Solution method}
\label{sec:method}

The direct MILP is tractable only for truncated candidate sets: the
linearization adds $|I| \times |K|$ auxiliaries per fire and scenario,
over 650{,}000 per fire-scenario pair at the full catalog. We therefore
solve the full problem with a fix-and-optimize matheuristic
\citep{helber2010}, an improvement scheme based on mixed-integer
programming (MIP) and distinct from local
branching \citep{fischetti2003}, with large neighborhood search
\citep{pisinger2010} over the first-stage variables.

The incumbent is a first-stage solution $(y, w, n)$. Each iteration
selects a neighborhood of candidate sites to free, builds a reduced
model containing only the freed sites plus the incumbent's open sites,
fixes the non-freed open sites at their incumbent values, solves the
reduced sub-MILP exactly over the full scenario set, and accepts the
result if it strictly improves the incumbent. Dropping a site that is
closed in the incumbent and outside the neighborhood is exact, not an
approximation: such a site is mathematically identical to one with its
binary fixed at zero. Every accepted iteration is therefore a verified
improvement of the true objective, and the sub-MILP size is bounded by
the neighborhood, not the catalog.

Neighborhood selection follows Shaw's relatedness principle
\citep{pisinger2010} in geographic form: a random seed site anchors
each iteration, and the
nearest candidate bases and water points to it are freed together,
matching the problem's own coupling, since sites an aircraft would
cycle between are exactly the sites worth reconsidering jointly. Pure
geographic neighborhoods, however, provably admit local optima that no
number of iterations escapes: when the only improving move must close
an open base and open a distant one within the same iteration, for
example when the budget affords a single aircraft so relocation is the
only option, no single seed's nearest-sites neighborhood ever contains
both. We observed exactly this on a real instance, where the optimal
base lay 174 km from the incumbent's and 200 geographic iterations
produced no improvement. Following standard adaptive large neighborhood
search practice \citep{ropke2006}, each iteration is therefore, with
probability $\pi$, a random destroy that frees sites drawn uniformly
from the whole catalog; unit tests reproduce the trap instance and
confirm the random operator escapes it reliably across seeds. A
parameter sweep on the base-case instance with a known optimum (24
configurations, five seeds each, 100 iterations, early stopping
disabled so reliability is not confounded with patience)
discriminates sharply: pure geographic destroy missed the optimum in
three of its six configurations (hit rates 0.6 to 0.8, one run
stalling far above the optimum), while configurations with destroy
probability 0.2 or higher reached it in 89 of 90 runs. Reliability,
not only the synthetic trap, mandates the random operator. Since
every mixed configuration attains the optimum, neighborhood sizes
are chosen for runtime, five bases and ten water points per
iteration, the fastest reliable configuration at about 22 seconds
per 100-iteration run, and $\pi = 0.3$ sits inside the reliable
band.

\section{Computational study}
\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments run on an Intel Core i7-13620H with 16 GB RAM under
Windows 11, with Gurobi 13.0.2 as the MIP solver (PuLP 3.3.2 as the
modeling layer). Unless stated otherwise, instances use the base-case
parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params}, candidate subsets are taken in
catalog order, and scenario subsets renormalize probabilities. Two
reading conventions apply throughout. Arrival times
$t^{\mathrm{arr}}_f$ are recomputed over each instance's truncated
candidate base subset, so the requirement is defined consistently
within every instance. And because $c^{\mathrm{wat}}_k$ enters only
the budget constraint, a solution with slack budget carries
alternate-optimal open water points at no objective cost, so the
count of open water points in any reported solution is bookkeeping
noise, never a finding. Every table and figure is generated by a
committed script from a committed results file.

\subsection{Tractability and the matheuristic}
\label{sec:exp2}

Figure~\ref{fig:runtime} shows how direct tractability degrades as
the fleet regime grows. Single-aircraft instances at 20 bases, 50
water points, and 10 scenarios (81 fires) solve to proven optimality
in 12 to 17 minutes. The two-aircraft regime is borderline: at a
tight budget (160 G, leaving 8 G of slack) the direct solve proves
optimality in about ten minutes, while with more slack (225 G) it
still carries a 0.5 percent gap at the 1,800 s limit. At three
aircraft (300 G) that limit leaves an essentially 100 percent gap, a
lower bound near zero; an earlier unlimited two-aircraft probe, run
before the containment units were corrected, had already burned 16
wall-clock hours without proof, which is what fixed the 1,800 s
protocol used throughout. What drives the cliff is budget slack and
the configuration symmetry it buys as much as fleet size itself,
cheap bases alone leave a 69 percent gap at the same limit

integrated question matters.

\subsection{Integrated versus sequential siting}
\label{sec:exp3}

The star comparison asks what joint optimization is worth against a
sequential planner who sites bases first and water second. Our
sequential baseline is deliberately strong. Phase A solves the same
model with a water-blind relaxation, every fire is assumed to reach
its best-case water point in the full catalog at zero cost, under a
budget share $\phi B$ for bases and fleet; phase B fixes phase A's
bases and fleet and solves the full model, real water catalog and
costs, real total budget, deciding only water points and recourse. We
sweep the split $\phi$ and report the best sequential result, so the
comparison is against a best-case sequential planner, not a straw man.
By construction the sequential solution is feasible for the integrated
model, so it can never win; on a hand-built instance in the companion
test suite it is strictly worse at every $\phi$, because a water-blind
phase A commits its single affordable aircraft toward a marginally
more valuable fire whose only water point is economically out of
reach, a misdirection no budget split can undo.

On the real Cundinamarca instances the result is an honest null with a
sharp structural explanation. Across every configuration tested,
single-aircraft budgets of 78, 80, 90, and 150 G COP, water costs at
both 50 M and the top-of-range 300 M, two and ten scenarios, up to 20
bases and 50 water points, and now also the two-aircraft budget of
160 G, which the corrected instance solves to proven optimality in
about ten minutes, the best sequential solution matches the
integrated optimum to solver tolerance (at 90 G the sequential value
even lands 0.005 percent below the integrated incumbent, which
stopped at the solver's default relative gap; tolerance noise, not a
real gap). The reason is regime structure: with $c^{\mathrm{air}} =
75$ G, any budget in $[76, 152)$ G fields exactly one aircraft;
constraint \eqref{eq:fleet} then serves at most one fire per
scenario, so at most $|S|$ distinct water points are ever needed, and
even the tightest leftover, 2 G at $B = 78$ G, buys the handful that
matter. Water cannot compete for budget, and a water-blind base
choice is as good as an informed one. The $\phi$ sweep exposes the
mechanism directly: every split that cannot fund the aircraft
($\phi < 0.975$ at 78 G) collapses to the all-escape plan, and every
split that can reproduces the same optimum. The baseline also
recommends itself as a warm start, reaching the 160 G optimum in 36
seconds against 618 for the direct integrated solve. The honest
headline is conditional: sequential siting is optimal or
indistinguishable from optimal in every regime we can certify, one
and two aircraft alike; the hand-built counterexample shows the gap
becomes strictly positive when a single decisive water point is
economically out of a water-blind planner's reach; and the regime
where the integrated model plausibly matters, three-plus aircraft
under large budget slack, is exactly the one the direct MILP cannot
certify (Sections~\ref{sec:exp2} and~\ref{sec:exp6}). We therefore
measured that regime against the strongest integrated search
available instead of leaving it bounded: at 300 G the matheuristic,
run cold and re-run warm-started from the sequential plan itself
(150 iterations, about half an hour each), lands on the sequential
value to solver tolerance, as does the direct solver's own 1,800 s
incumbent, four independent solution paths agreeing at 14,478.11.
What is missing in the three-aircraft regime is the certificate,
whose lower bound configuration symmetry destroys, not a better
plan.

\subsection{Risk attitude}
\label{sec:exp4}

Figure~\ref{fig:exp4} sweeps the mean-risk weight $\lambda$ on the
20-base, 50-water, 10-scenario instance. The efficient frontier is
degenerate: expected loss and CVaR are both flat from $\lambda = 0$ to
$0.75$, because a single affordable aircraft cannot hedge the worst
day at all, there is no tail-protection to buy. At $\lambda = 1$, pure
CVaR, the solver returns a dominated plan: identical CVaR, 54 percent
worse expected loss, six more escaped fires, since a pure tail
objective is indifferent to everything off the tail; every plan with
the same tail value is optimal at $\lambda = 1$, and which one the
solver returns is arbitrary, exactly the degeneracy a lexicographic
or epsilon-regularized objective would remove. This is the
textbook argument for the mean-risk combination over pure CVaR,
reproduced on real data. With ten equiprobable scenarios,
$\CVaR_{0.95}$ equals the worst-scenario loss, the expected
small-sample behavior under SAA, which the replication analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:saa} quantifies.

\subsection{SAA replication and optimality gap}
\label{sec:saa}

How much of the ten-scenario results is sampling noise? We follow the
replication protocol of \citet{kleywegt2001}: $M = 10$ independent
bootstrap replications of $N = 10$ day-scenarios each, all ten solved
to proven optimality within the limit and none excluded, whose mean
optimal value estimates a statistical lower bound on the true
optimum, 10{,}505 with a 95 percent $t$-interval of $\pm$6{,}074. The
interval is wide because day-to-day fire load varies enormously
(replication optima range from 41 to 22{,}716), which is exactly the
variance a day-scenario model must carry. The ten candidate
first-stage plans, one per replication, are then evaluated on an
independent 100-scenario sample; the best is re-evaluated on a
second, fresh 100-scenario sample, so that selecting the minimum does
not flatter the bound. The selected plan's fresh-sample mean-risk
value is 10{,}876 (expectation component 2{,}631 $\pm$ 1{,}008; CVaR
component 19{,}121, a point estimate), giving an optimality-gap point
estimate of 371, or 3.4 percent of the upper bound.

Two further readings matter. First, selection is what makes the
bound tight: the pre-registered naive candidate, the first
replication's plan, which happened to be optimized against an
unusually quiet draw, evaluates to a 69 percent gap, while four of
the ten independently drawn replications evaluate to the identical
mean-risk value on the selection sample, so SAA at $N = 10$ already
stabilizes the first-stage deployment even though it cannot certify
it. Second, the CVaR component is noisy at this evaluation size: the
same selected plan's sample CVaR moved from 35{,}947 on the selection
sample to 19{,}121 on the fresh one, which is why the reported upper
bound comes from the fresh sample only and why the gap carries a
formal confidence interval only on its expectation component. The
downward small-sample bias of sample CVaR loosens, without
invalidating, the statistical lower bound.

\subsection{Sensitivity: fleet first, containment physics second}
\label{sec:exp6}

A one-at-a-time sweep around the base case varies seven axes: budget,
window, $A_0$, $c$, $\rho_{\mathrm{scale}}$, and both site costs, 21
solves in total, each under a 1,800 s limit; four points did not
reach proof and are reported as incumbents with their remaining gaps
(the two multi-aircraft budgets, the four-hour window, and the
cheapest base cost). The result has a clear hierarchy.

Budget moves the objective most (Figure~\ref{fig:exp6}): 225 G fields
two aircraft and improves the base case by 18 percent (incumbent at a
0.5 percent gap), and 300 G fields three for a 35 percent improvement,
though there the remaining gap is essentially 100 percent, the
tractability cliff of Section~\ref{sec:exp2}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{fig_exp6_budget}
\caption{Experiment 6, budget axis: the dominant sensitivity axis.
Annotations give the fleet size fielded. Open markers are 1,800 s
incumbents (upper bounds on the optimum).}
\label{fig:exp6}
\end{figure}

The containment constants now matter too, and they matter as a cliff
rather than a dial. The base case contains 7 of 81 fires: one
aircraft, one fire per day-scenario, and not every day's best fire is
containable. Halving the window to one hour loses containments
(objective up 6 percent); stretching it to four hours gains them
(incumbent 16 percent below the base case, at a 2.5 percent gap) and
then saturates, since the eight-hour point, solved to proven
optimality, lands on exactly the same value: once the served fire's
requirement fits inside the window, constraint \eqref{eq:fleet}, not
time, is what binds. $A_0$ and $c$ sweep the system across its
containability cliff: at $A_0 = 5$ ha, or 14 ha, or $c = 10$
L/m$^2$, essentially nothing is containable by a single Firehawk (80
to 81 of 81 fires escape and the objective sits at or within 0.05
percent of the all-escape value, with the 14 ha solution opening
nothing at all), while at $A_0 = 0.1$ ha or $c = 1.5$ the objective
saturates at the same floor as the long windows, the value of serving
the best containable fire each day. Doubling
$\rho_{\mathrm{scale}}$ raises expected loss by 20 percent while
leaving the worst day untouched: spread rate degrades the average,
not the tail this fleet cannot hedge anyway.

Site costs remain the one axis that never moves the objective across
its full documented range, the surviving piece of the pre-correction
reading. Cheap bases do, however, destroy tractability rather than
economics: at 300 M per base the solver still carried a 69 percent
gap at its limit while its incumbent matched the proven optimum of
every other cost point, pure configuration symmetry.

The policy reading is sharper than a flat sensitivity table. At these
prices, marginal pesos buy more protection as aircraft with their
basing than as any refinement of the ground network; but whether an
aircraft buys anything at all is decided by the containment regime,
that is, by how large fires are when attack begins, which argues for
pairing any acquisition with faster detection-to-dispatch. All of it
must be read alongside the disclosed no-intra-day-reuse
simplification, which understates single-aircraft capacity and
correspondingly overstates the marginal value of additional
aircraft.

\subsection{The plan on the ground}
\label{sec:ground}

Solved plans are useful to a planner only as places, so we close the
study by reading the base-case optimum operationally (the full
solution report is generated by a committed script). The plan opens
one base, the Cerritos aerodrome, about 79 km west of Bogot{\'a}
toward the Magdalena valley, stations the single affordable Firehawk
there, and enables water points of which exactly six are ever used:
the La Regadera reservoir, Lake Guatavita, the Pantano Redondo and
Encatada lagoons, the Romero well (used on two of the ten days), and
one waterfall pool, each a named, existing water body from the
OpenStreetMap and CAR catalogs. Across the ten day-scenarios the
aircraft serves the highest-exposure containable fire of each day,
seven containments in all, with delivery margins between 1 and 30
percent of the requirement (30,283 liters delivered against a 30,000
liter requirement on the tightest day): the water-cycle coupling is
the binding margin of every single containment, not a theoretical
nicety. The worst scenario in the CVaR tail is the real day of 15
January 2024, eighteen simultaneous fires, the very episode in whose
wake the Firehawk acquisition was announced.

Three operational readings follow, stated with the caveat that
several bases tie as alternate optima (Cerritos, Colombaima, and
aerodromes as far as Sogamoso and Canaima appear across equivalent
runs). First, what is stable across every tie is not the aerodrome
but the served fires and the water points that serve them: the six
water bodies above cost about 300 M COP in total at the swept
enabling cost, 0.4 percent of one aircraft, so securing legal and
logistical dip access to a short list of named reservoirs and
lagoons is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost action available, and
it is actionable before any aircraft arrives. Second, because the
base choice is degenerate, the aerodrome can be selected on criteria
the model does not price, existing infrastructure, maintenance
co-location, military logistics, at no measured loss of protection,
provided the water list comes with it. Third, the containability
cliff of Section~\ref{sec:exp6} translates into dispatch doctrine:
every containment in the plan happens because the aircraft attacks
the day's best containable fire within the two-hour window, and at
attack sizes above five hectares nothing is containable, so the
value of the acquisition rides on detection-to-dispatch speed as
much as on where anything is sited.

\section{Conclusions, limitations, and future research}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We introduced the first model that jointly sites aerial suppression
bases and water refill points with cycle-endogenous aircraft
productivity, under a two-stage stochastic program with a mean-risk
CVaR objective, gave it an exact linearization and a verified-exact
fix-and-optimize matheuristic, and built a fully sourced open-data
case study for Cundinamarca around Colombia's real Firehawk
acquisition. The computational study's contribution is structural: a
fleet-first, physics-second regime in which sequential siting is
indistinguishable from optimal in every certifiable configuration;
containment constants that act as a containability cliff rather than
as nuisance parameters, so the model separates the fire regimes an
acquisition can and cannot defend; site costs that never bind; a
tractability cliff driven by fleet size and budget slack; CVaR
degeneracies that argue for the mean-risk form on real data; and an
operational reading in real places, a one-aircraft plan whose
protection rides on a short, nearly free list of named water bodies
and on attack speed rather than on the specific aerodrome chosen
(Section~\ref{sec:ground}).

Limitations are disclosed throughout and summarized here. The
containment law is a phenomenological exponential with uncalibrated,
swept constants; scenarios cover a single year's bootstrap pool, so
interannual variability is unrepresented; each scenario is a
no-reuse episode, understating per-aircraft daily capacity; the
aircraft type is single; site costs are uniform sweep points; and
water points are uncapacitated, with no volume depletion or
simultaneous-use congestion, a simplification that matters most
precisely in the multi-aircraft regimes where the integrated model's
value concentrates. The one open computational question is
certification in the three-aircraft, high-budget-slack regime: four
independent solution paths agree there to solver tolerance
(Section~\ref{sec:exp3}), but none proves optimality, because budget
slack destroys the direct lower bound through configuration
symmetry; symmetry breaking or stronger bounds for that regime are
the natural next step.
